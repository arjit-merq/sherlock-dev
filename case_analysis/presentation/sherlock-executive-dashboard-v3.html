What changed

How Sherlock investigates today, and why accuracy rose as the work got harder.

1

It now looks where an engineer looks

Airflow onlyearly investigations
→
The full picturehow it works now

At the start Sherlock mostly read Airflow: did the DAG fail, what did the log say. That is one slice of a real ticket. It now follows the same trail a PE follows — index manifests, databases, prior tickets, Slack threads, and GitHub PRs — and checks them against each other instead of trusting a single log line.

2

It got better by doing more real work

20tickets in 15 Aug – 29 Aug
→
34tickets in 14 Sep – 18 Sep

In plain terms: practice on live PES tickets. Across the period it investigated 76 of them, and the newest window is the largest. Every closed case was fed back in — prior tickets, write-ups, and playbooks — so the next run could reuse what already worked instead of starting from scratch. The later tickets were not the easy ones — vendor gaps, index config, reports, options pipelines. Accuracy rose while the pile got bigger and messier, not smaller and simpler.

3

Each case leaves a recipe for the next

8playbooks on 15 Aug
→
74playbooks on 18 Sep

When a case closes, the failure class is written down so the next similar ticket does not start from zero. That is why more cases help: Sherlock can reuse what already worked, then spend its time on what is actually new. The later window was also graded harder — 26 of 34 runs could not see the ticket's resolution comments, versus {first_blind} of {first_total} at the start — so the gain is not from reading the answer.

Where it still gets it wrong

4 of 76 scored tickets got a different root cause than the engineer — 2 in 15 Aug – 29 Aug, 1 in 30 Aug – 13 Sep, 1 in 14 Sep – 18 Sep.

All four share one pattern: each was closed by a human fact that exists nowhere in the logs or the code — the job actually succeeded, the data was fine and someone overrode it, the ticket named the wrong report, a refresh was agreed in a side thread. Sherlock is strong at diagnosing systems and weak at recognising when there is no defect to diagnose.

The common mistake is accepting the ticket at face value. In all four it took the reported problem as true and moved straight to explaining it, instead of first checking whether the job actually failed, whether the data was actually wrong, or whether the object named in the ticket was the live one. The systems reasoning was usually sound; the step it skipped was testing the premise.

Every one was delivered at 90% confidence or higher. That is the part worth fixing: a wrong answer stated confidently is harder for an engineer to catch than a hedged one. Calibrating confidence, and letting Sherlock conclude “nothing is broken”, is the next piece of work.

PES-1165 please help to run the report stated 93% confident
What actually closed it

Nothing had failed. The report ran, the engineer confirmed delivery, and the requester replied “I got the report thanks”.

What Sherlock said

Described a full-history load exhausting memory and the pod being killed around May 2020.

Where it went wrong

It saw a scheduling event on the way to a successful run and treated it as proof of a crash. It never checked the end state — did the job finish, did the mail go out — before building the explanation. Both answers were sitting in the ticket thread. Reading “help me run this” as “something is broken” is the error; a request for help is not an incident.

PES-1120 2007 SI settlement prices wrong stated 91% confident
What actually closed it

Our prices were correct and matched Barchart. The requester preferred Bloomberg’s values and applied manual overrides.

What Sherlock said

Went looking for a settlement-versus-close data defect, assuming something was broken.

Where it went wrong

The reporter said the prices were wrong, and Sherlock accepted that and went hunting for the cause. The engineer did the opposite: checked our numbers against an outside source first, found they matched, and closed it as a preference for Bloomberg. On a disputed-data ticket the first move is to verify the claim, not to explain it.

PES-1144 DIA Fair Strike Calculation stated 90% confident
What actually closed it

Settled in conversation: the DIA seed dividends had been refreshed, so historical vol surfaces were wiped and backloaded from 2012.

What Sherlock said

Blamed a delta-filter formula defect in the fair-strike calculation.

Where it went wrong

It found something odd in the calculation code and stopped there, without asking why the numbers changed when the formula had not. The cause was upstream data being regenerated, which a check on when the inputs were last rebuilt would have surfaced. This one is also a step backwards: an earlier run of the same ticket scored 90.

PES-1142 Error while Updating the "indicative_report_email" Report stated 97% confident
What actually closed it

The ticket quoted a report that had been paused since 2022. The live report was a different one, and its recipients were simply updated.

What Sherlock said

Diagnosed a schema validation failure — sound reasoning, but about the wrong report.

Where it went wrong

The analysis itself was correct; it was performed on the wrong object. Sherlock took the ID in the ticket as fact and never asked whether that report was the one in use. The evidence was there — paused since 2022, no recent runs — and would have redirected the whole investigation. It validated the artifact it was handed instead of the premise behind it.

Where it was close

11 of 76 scored tickets were in the right area but not the mechanism that closed the ticket — 5 in 15 Aug – 29 Aug, 3 in 30 Aug – 13 Sep, 3 in 14 Sep – 18 Sep.

These are not invented defects. Sherlock named the right system and often the right failure class. What it missed is the last step: the specific bug, the fix that actually shipped, or a process decision (wait, switch vendor, point at the other region).

Confidence is still high — 86% to 97%. Same problem as the wrong calls, in a milder form: a near-miss delivered as a finished diagnosis. The next piece of work is to separate “right neighborhood” from “this is the close”.

How this is scored. Sherlock's stated root cause is compared against the engineer comments and resolution that actually closed each Jira ticket. Correct means the same mechanism; partial means the right area but an incomplete cause; wrong means a different mechanism. Tickets with no engineer diagnosis on record are omitted from this page. Windows are keyed to the date Sherlock ran, so each bar reflects the build that was live at the time.

What this does not claim. Each window holds 20–34 scored tickets, so treat the individual percentages as directional rather than precise. Ticket counts, playbooks, and blind runs are measured. How Sherlock investigates (Airflow, then manifests, databases, Slack, GitHub, prior cases) is how the current runs are wired, not a controlled experiment.
